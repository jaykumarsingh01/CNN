How a CNN Sees a Cat

We take one real cat photo and follow the exact information it carries as it flows through every layer of a Convolutional Neural Network — pixel by pixel, channel by channel, until the network says: "cat 🐱".

STEP 01

The Input — A Real Cat, but the Computer Sees Numbers

To us this is a photo of an orange tabby. To the CNN it's a tensor of numbers of shape H × W × 3. Each pixel stores three integers (0–255): how much Red, Green, and Blue light it emits. Nothing else. No labels, no "cat" tag — just numbers.

💡 Bright areas in a channel = that color is strong there. The cat's orange fur lights up strongly in Red and Green, but is dark in Blue — that's literally what "orange" means in RGB.
3 channels stacked into one tensor
STEP 02

Why Three Channels? What Each One Actually Carries

The eye (and the camera sensor) samples light through 3 color filters. The CNN keeps them separate because each channel carries different information about the world:

  • Red — warm tones. Strong on fur, tongue, skin, brick, sunset. In our cat: the whole body glows.
  • Green — mid-spectrum. Strong on grass, leaves, and (surprisingly) skin/fur too — orange = red + green.
  • Blue — cool tones. Strong on sky, water, denim, shadows. Our orange cat is dark here — that darkness is a clue "not sky, not blue object".
🎯 Why keep them split? Because a filter that fires on "orange fur" needs to see "high R + high G + low B" together. If we averaged channels into grayscale first, we'd throw away the color contrast that helps the CNN separate a cat from, say, a gray rock of the same brightness.
🧠 First conv layer kernels have shape 3×3×3 — 3 spatial × 3 channels. They look at all three colors at the same pixel and combine them with learned weights.
R, G, B — separate views of the same cat
STEP 03

Convolution — Sliding Filters Detect Local Patterns

A small kernel (say 3×3×3) slides across the cat. At every position it does one thing: multiply each pixel by a weight, add them all up → one number in the feature map.

  • An edge kernel gives a big number where brightness suddenly changes → outlines the cat's silhouette, ears, whiskers.
  • A color-blob kernel gives a big number on patches of orange fur.
  • A vertical-line kernel lights up on whiskers and fur strands.
🎯 Exact information flowing: the output is no longer "colors" — it's "where in the image does this specific pattern appear?" A whole layer has, say, 32 such kernels → we get 32 feature maps, each highlighting a different pattern in the cat.
🔍 The glowing purple cube is the kernel scanning the cat photo. The map below grows one cell at a time — that's a feature map being born.
3×3 kernel sliding over the cat
STEP 04

ReLU — Keep Evidence, Throw Away Doubt

A kernel can output negatives ("this pattern is anti-present here"). ReLU (max(0, x)) sets those to zero. Why?

  • Negative values would cancel out positive evidence in the next layer.
  • Zero means "no signal" — cleaner input for the next kernel.
  • It adds non-linearity, letting the network learn shapes that aren't just weighted sums.
🎯 Exact information kept: only "the pattern IS here, and this strongly". A whisker detector will now have a bright spot exactly where whiskers exist, and pure zero everywhere else.
⚡ Blue bars (negatives) collapse to the floor. Orange bars (positives) survive — those are the surviving votes for cat features.
ReLU activation
STEP 05

Pooling — Shrink While Keeping the Strongest Evidence

Max pooling takes the biggest number in each 2×2 window and drops the rest. On the cat that means: "in this little patch, an ear-tip detector fired strongly somewhere — I don't need to know the exact pixel, just that it happened here."

🎯 Why this matters for a cat: if the cat moves 2 pixels to the left in the next photo, the same ear-tip still lands in the same pooled window. Pooling gives the CNN translation robustness — the answer doesn't change if the cat shifts slightly.
📉 4×4 → 2×2. The tallest bar in each window "wins" and moves down into the pooled layer.
2×2 max pooling
STEP 06

Stacking Layers — From Edges to "Cat Face"

Repeat Conv → ReLU → Pool many times. The feature maps get smaller in width/height but deeper (more channels, each a more abstract concept):

  • Layer 1: edges, color blobs.
  • Layer 2: fur texture, corners, whisker segments.
  • Layer 3: eye shape, ear triangle, nose.
  • Layer 4: whole cat face — a single neuron that lights up when it sees a feline muzzle.
🎯 Information flow: spatial → semantic. Early channels answer "what's here?"; deep channels answer "what is this?"
🏗️ Watch each cube: it gets narrower (less spatial detail) but glows in different colors (more concepts stored).
Deep feature hierarchy
STEP 07

Flatten & Fully Connected — Weighing All the Evidence

The last 3D feature block is flattened into one long vector — imagine 512 numbers like [eye:0.9, whisker:0.8, ear-triangle:0.95, wheel:0.02, fin:0.01, ...].

Fully-connected (dense) layers combine these votes: every neuron in the next layer takes a weighted sum of all features and decides "does this evidence look like a cat, a dog, a car…?"

🎯 This is where the CNN says: high eye + high whisker + high ear-triangle → strong cat signal; presence of wheels → strong car signal. The weights were learned during training on millions of images.
🔗 Glowing lines = information flow. Brighter line = stronger weighted contribution.
Flatten → dense voting
STEP 08

Softmax — "It's a Cat, 94% Sure"

The final layer produces one raw score per class. Softmax squashes them into probabilities that sum to 1.0. The tallest bar is the network's answer.

🎯 For our orange tabby: Cat 0.94, Dog 0.03, Fox 0.02, Rabbit 0.005, Other 0.005. All those neurons and channels reduced billions of pixel arithmetic operations into one number: the cat probability.
🏆 The winning bar glows — that's the network's final vote after everything you just saw.
